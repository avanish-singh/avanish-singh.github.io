Avanish Kumar Singh

I teach neural networks to solve the equations that govern physical systems.

From electrons in a transistor to heat flowing through a chip, physical systems are described by partial differential equations. Solving them numerically means discretizing the problem and iterating until the solution settles. Depending on the problem, that takes anywhere from seconds to hours per case, and sometimes it never converges. I build machine-learning solvers that learn the converged answer, so it comes back in a fraction of a second.

My testbed is semiconductor devices. In Dr. Oves Badami's group I develop ML-based TCAD tools for FinFETs and stacked-nanosheet devices, and I write the solvers that produce the training data, from 1D Schrödinger–Poisson up to 3D quantum transport. The same methods carry over to other PDE-governed problems, such as heat conduction in 3D chip packages. This page is where I post what I'm working on.

Hybrid physics-informed neural solver for quantum-corrected simulation of 3D FETs

Trained networks replace the slow, iterative quantum-corrected simulation of FinFETs and stacked nanosheets, returning spatial profiles and drain current in a single forward pass.

The problem

Simulating modern transistors such as FinFETs and stacked nanosheets accurately is slow and fragile. Conventional TCAD meshes the device and iterates the coupled Schrödinger–Poisson and transport equations until they converge. That takes seconds to minutes per bias point, and once quantum confinement is included it sometimes fails to converge at all. Exploring a large design space this way is expensive.

The approach

A hybrid framework: neural networks learn the parts that are expensive to solve, and well-understood physics turns their outputs into terminal current. It takes three groups of inputs.

  • BiasingGate and drain voltages
  • Device geometrySemiconductor body and oxide thickness
  • Material parametersEffective masses, capturing crystal orientation and strain
  1. Spatial-profile networks predict the full electron-density profile n3D(x) and the electrostatic-potential profile V(x) across the channel cross-section at low drain bias, in one pass and with no iteration.
  2. Barrier network predicts the source–channel barrier at zero drain bias (φZD) and at the applied drain bias (φAD). As drain voltage increases, the drain's electric field reaches into the channel and lowers the barrier. This is drain-induced barrier lowering, and the network captures how much the barrier drops for each bias combination.
  3. Physics-based current module integrates the density into a line charge density, nLD = ∫ n3D dx. More carriers flow over a lowered barrier, so the charge is scaled exponentially by the barrier reduction and combined with a mobility model that accounts for doping and electric-field degradation:
IDS = q · nLD · exp[q(φZD − φAD)kBT]· μ(V, Elat) · Elat

Why it stays physical

The networks are trained to respect physical behavior, not only to fit numbers. They reproduce how the device responds to changes in bias and geometry, and they obey the expected gate-control trends. The current itself comes from an interpretable physical model rather than a black box, so the predictions hold up beyond the conditions seen in training.

What it enables

  • Full I–V sweeps in a fraction of a second, with no convergence failures.
  • Fast exploration across thickness, bias, orientation and strain.

Graph neural networks · TCADOngoing

Graph network for double-gate MOS electrostatics

Each TCAD potential profile becomes a graph, one node per mesh point across the oxide–silicon–oxide stack, and an edge-aware message-passing network predicts the potential at every node. A Sobolev loss makes it match the field and curvature too. Training covers 464 profiles, 3–26 nm films at 0.6–1.2 V. Next I'm testing an edge-free baseline to see how much the message passing itself adds.

Reinforcement learning · numerical methodsOngoing

Reinforcement learning that makes a quantum electrostatic solver converge faster

The conventional 1D Poisson–Schrödinger solver for a double-gate stack stays exactly as it is: the same Newton update for Poisson's equation, the same Schrödinger eigenproblem and carrier model, iterated to self-consistency. Reinforcement learning only sets the starting conditions that decide how quickly that loop converges.

  • Initial potential V(x). A parametric profile with a linear drop across each oxide and a parabolic well in the channel, set by two numbers, so the loop starts close to the answer.
  • Graded mesh. Fine near the oxide–silicon interfaces and coarser where the solution is flat. The finest spacing is pinned to the conventional one, so the inversion layer is never under-resolved.

The agent sees the gate bias and channel thickness and makes one decision per solve, which makes it a contextual bandit trained with PPO (γ = 0). Its reward is the log of the work saved, but only when the solve passes an accuracy check against a tightly converged reference. Any inaccurate solve scores below every accurate one, so speed is never bought with accuracy.

To support graded meshes I rewrote the solver with a variable-spacing finite-volume Poisson scheme and a symmetrized Schrödinger operator. On a uniform mesh it reproduces the conventional code to about 10−10 V.

Why I changed the approach

The project began as inverse design: find the channel thickness that produces a target on-current. Gate voltage dominates the inversion charge and thickness is a weak secondary effect, so many geometries matched the same current and the reward was flat and noisy. Instead of asking RL to invent a design the physics doesn't pin down, I now use it to help a trusted simulator run faster.
